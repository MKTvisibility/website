Learn what your customers are trying to solve

MKT Visibility · October 27, 2026

Two customers may buy the same product for different reasons. One buys a locally made gift because they need something meaningful. Another buys it because they need something available for pickup this afternoon. The item is identical; the information that helps each person decide is different.
Good audience research looks for those situations. It should help you serve real people rather than invent elaborate personalities based on assumptions.

Start with evidence you already have

Review common questions, inquiry emails, staff observations, and genuine customer feedback. Look for recurring concerns about price, timing, accessibility, fit, location, or how the service works. Ask customers what helped them choose and what almost stopped them. A handful of conversations can generate useful ideas, although it cannot establish what every customer thinks.

Separate a known fact from a guess. “Four recent inquiries asked about evening appointments” is an observation. “Our entire audience wants evening appointments” is a conclusion that needs more evidence. This distinction prevents a marketing plan from becoming a collection of confident assumptions.

Write a customer situation

For a hypothetical Colorado attraction: “A visitor is choosing an activity for tomorrow, needs to understand the time commitment, and is unsure whether advance booking is required.” That sentence suggests useful content: duration, what to bring, booking instructions, and weather-related updates.

For a professional service: “An owner knows they need help but wants to understand the process before agreeing to a call.” That suggests a process article, a written inquiry option, and a clear explanation of what happens next.

How MKT approaches the work

Audience discovery should turn owner knowledge into usable themes. Collect the questions customers actually ask, identify the purchase situations that matter most, and match each situation to a message and next step. Revisit the assumptions when inquiries change or a new service launches.

This approach also informs website writing. Google recommends useful, reliable content intended for people rather than material produced mainly to attract search traffic. *Source: Google's people-first content guidance.

Try it yourself

Write down the last ten questions customers asked. Group similar questions. Pick one that could prevent a sale or visit if it remains unanswered. Turn it into a short, accurate explanation with a clear next step. Avoid sharing the customer's identity or private circumstances.

Where professional help adds value

Owners often know their customers well but lack time to organize that knowledge into repeatable marketing. MKT can help identify patterns, develop content from those patterns, and check whether the response supports the original assumption.

Start with the customer's situation. The best topic may already be sitting in your inbox.
